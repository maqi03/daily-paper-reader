# Can LLMs Be Constrained to the Past? Improving Knowledge Cutoff through Recall-Based Prompting 

Michiro Asai, Ailiang Lin, Yu Kishimoto, Takao Obi, Satoshi Kosugi, Kotaro Funakoshi , Manabu Okumura 

Institute of Science Tokyo 

{asai,linailiang,ky,obi,kosugi,funakoshi,oku}@lr.first.iir.isct.ac.jp 

Abstract 

Prompted knowledge cutoff instructs a large language model (LLM) to act as if informa-tion beyond a specified cutoff date were un-available. However, prior work mainly relies on direct-answer generation, which struggles when post-cutoff knowledge is not explicitly queried but is only causally related to the ques-tion. To address this limitation, we propose two recall-based prompting strategies: Self-Recall (SR), which asks the model to restate its cutoff constraint, and Question-Recall (QR), which requires the model to recall question-relevant information valid under the cutoff. Across three existing benchmarks, our methods outperform both direct-answer prompting and conventional step-by-step reasoning baselines, with particu-larly strong improvements on counterfactual questions. To investigate robustness across different cutoff settings, we further construct the Multi-cutoff Historical Event Benchmark (MHEB), which evaluates the same question under multiple cutoff years. Results show that knowledge cutoff performance varies with cut-off distance, while combining SR and QR con-sistently yields the best performance. 

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly applied to temporal prediction tasks such as stock price forecasting, climate modeling, and traffic pre-diction (Yu et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2024). However, LLMs are pretrained on large-scale corpora col-lected over long time spans, raising a fundamental concern of data contamination: evaluation on ex-amples predating the model’s actual knowledge cut-off may probe memorized knowledge rather than genuine temporal reasoning (Roberts et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2024). Therefore, constraining an LLM to answer based only on knowledge available be-fore a specified cutoff date is critical to ensuring trustworthy temporal benchmarking and preventing knowledge leakage in real-world applications. To ensure temporal consistency, prior work 1 typ-ically relies on additional training over curated temporal datasets (Ren et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024). While these methods effectively improve temporal grounding, they require internal model access, and thus cannot be applied to black-box LLMs served through APIs. In contrast, Prompted Knowledge Cutoff (Gao et al., 2025) instructs an LLM to unlearn all information beyond a specific cutoff date through prompting, e.g., “Assume you are an AI with a knowledge cutoff of December 31, 2020.” While this approach is effective when the forgotten information is explicitly queried, its per-formance degrades significantly in cases where that information is only causally related to the question. Another limitation is that existing prompted-cutoff benchmarks assign a single fixed cutoff date to each question. This setup reveals whether a prompt succeeds at one particular temporal boundary, but not how performance varies as the cutoff is shifted further back from the event year. To this end, we propose two novel recall-based prompting strategies that generate intermediate out-puts to improve knowledge cutoff performance: a 

Self-Recall (SR) prompt that encourages the model to output one short statement of its cutoff stance before answering, recasting the temporal constraint from an external instruction into a self-declared state that is more likely to persist through gener-ation; and a Question-Recall (QR) prompt that instructs the model to produce a short overview of question-relevant facts together with their associ-ated years before answering, anchoring retrieval to the specified cutoff and suppressing activation of post-cutoff information. For evaluation, we fur-ther construct a Multi-cutoff Historical Event Benchmark (MHEB) , in which each instance is grounded in a historical event from the 13th to 21st century. 2 Methodology 

2.1 Self-Recall and Question-Recall 

While prompting methods that generate interme-diate outputs, such as step-by-step reasoning, are known to improve LLM performance on various tasks (Kojima et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023), prior work (Gao et al., 2025) on prompted knowledge cutoff has only examined direct-answer prompt-ing. We close this gap by introducing two novel recall-based prompting strategies, Self-Recall and Question-Recall, as shown in Figure 1. 

Self-Recall (SR). Previous research suggests that prompting a model to first restate its assigned role can improve LLMs’ reasoning capabilities (Kong et al., 2024). Inspired by this finding, we propose SELF -R ECALL , which aims to strengthen role ad-herence under prompted knowledge cutoff. SR re-quires the model to explicitly recall its knowledge cutoff condition before generating the final answer. In this way, we convert the constraint from an ex-ternal user instruction into a self-declared internal state of the LLM, making the intended temporal boundary more likely to be reflected through sub-sequent generation. 

Question-Recall (QR). QUESTION -R ECALL in-corporates temporal context by instructing the model to first recall question-relevant facts avail-able at the specified cutoff date. Unlike simple fact enumeration, QR pairs each recalled fact with its temporal information, thereby organizing the re-trieved knowledge with respect to the cutoff bound-ary. This preliminary recall step is intended to an-chor knowledge retrieval at the cutoff and suppress the model’s reliance on post-cutoff information. 

Prompt Design. Both prompting methods are de-signed to control the model’s selection of existing knowledge rather than to enhance reasoning . As illustrated in Figure 1, we restrict the intermedi-ate recall output in both methods to a single short sentence, without imposing additional tasks on the model, such as excessive structuring, detailed ex-planations, or explicit reasoning. In addition to ap-plying SR and QR individually, we evaluate two or-dered combinations, SR →QR and QR →SR, to ex-amine whether combining role reinforcement and temporally grounded recall improves performance. 

2.2 Multi-cutoff Historical Event Benchmark 

Existing prompted knowledge cutoff bench-marks (Gao et al., 2025) fix each question’s cutoff year to the event year itself, leaving the effect of varying the cutoff unexamined. To address this, we construct the Multi-cutoff Historical Event Benchmark (MHEB) , which allows us to evaluate the generalization of prompted knowledge cutoff methods across different cutoff year settings. 

Construction. We collect historical events from Wikidata, focusing on entities labeled as war 

(Q198), battle (Q178561), or occurrence 

(Q1190554), each annotated with an occurrence year. For each candidate event, we prompt the tar-get LLM to identify its occurrence year without any cutoff instruction, and retain only the events answered correctly. This filtering step ensures that subsequent cutoff failures cannot be attributed to the model’s lack of factual knowledge. The final dataset contains 900 historical events, with 100 sampled from each century starting from the 13th century (1201–1300). 

Format. Each instance is formulated as a binary question, “Has a historical event called {event} ever occurred?” . For each event, the cutoff year can be set to varying offsets before the event year. When no cutoff is applied, the correct answer is “Yes”; when the cutoff year is at or before the event year, the correct answer is “No”. This design allows us to evaluate whether prompted cutoff remains effective as the cutoff year is shifted farther back from the event year. Further details about MHEB are provided in Appendix B.4.

Table 2 reports results on MHEB under different cutoff distances, with Y cutoff −Y event ranging from 

−9 to 0. For example, a value of −5 means that the cutoff is set five years before the event year, so the model should answer using only knowledge available before the event occurred. Although MHEB contains only events whose oc-currence years are correctly identified by the model without a cutoff prompt, knowledge cutoff still fails in many cases. This confirms that such failures are not due to missing event knowledge, but rather to the model’s difficulty in suppressing knowl-edge that should be unavailable under the speci-fied cutoff. Notably, SR →QR achieves the best performance across all cutoff settings, indicating that combining role reinforcement with question-specific recall effectively generalizes knowledge cutoff across different temporal distances. Moreover, we observe different trends across prompting methods. P1 and SR tend to achieve higher success rates as the cutoff moves farther away from the event year, suggesting that a larger temporal gap makes the cutoff constraint easier to maintain. In contrast, step-by-step prompting meth-ods often perform best when Y cutoff − Y event =0. Since these methods often generate question-related temporal information during intermediate reasoning, we hypothesize that such temporal cues are useful when the cutoff is close to the event year, but become less reliable as the cutoff is shifted farther back. 

4 Conclusion 

In this work, we proposed two recall-based prompts, Self-Recall (SR) and Question-Recall (QR), for prompted knowledge cutoff in LLMs. Experiments on three existing benchmarks across three LLMs show that our methods consistently outperform direct-answer and step-by-step prompt-ing, with the largest gains on counterfactual ques-tions where the relevant knowledge is only causally related to the question. To further investigate robustness under different cutoff settings, we in-troduce the Multi-cutoff Historical Event Bench-mark (MHEB). Notably, applying SR before QR achieves the best performance across the full range of cutoff offsets on MHEB. We hope this work offers a new perspective for improving prompted knowledge cutoff in LLMs. Limitations 

Due to computational constraints, our experiments focus on prompt-based methods and do not ex-plore unlearning techniques that require fine-tuning on additional datasets. In addition, all prompts and benchmarks used in this work are in English. Extending recall-based prompting to multilingual settings and constructing multilingual versions of MHEB to validate its effectiveness across lan-guages are left for future work.

A.1 Ensuring Temporal Consistency in LLMs 

LLMs are trained on corpora spanning long time periods, which can lead to temporally inconsistent knowledge recall. For example, given the question “In 2019, in what year was the Tokyo Olympics scheduled to be held?”, a temporally consistent model should answer “2020”, but may instead re-call the post-postponement outcome and answer “2021”. This mismatch arises when the time as-sumed by the question differs from the time point of the knowledge used by the model. Several studies have explored ways to align LLM responses with a target time. Dhingra et al. (2022) pretrain models on timestamped corpora and show that models can represent “time-conditioned knowl-edge”. Zhao et al. (2024) align model knowl-edge to a desired year through fine-tuning on tem-porally curated data. Park et al. (2025a) iden-tify attention heads specialized for recalling time-dependent knowledge, suggesting that temporal knowledge may be editable through internal inter-ventions. However, these methods require either additional training or internal model modifications, making them difficult to apply to closed-source LLMs served only through APIs. In contrast, Park et al. (2025b) propose ChroKnowPrompt, a prompt-ing method that aligns the model’s knowledge re-call with a specific year by using correct facts from neighboring years as few-shot examples (Brown et al., 2020). While this avoids parameter updates, it assumes access to reliable facts around the target year, which may not always be available. 

A.2 Machine Unlearning 

Machine unlearning aims to make a trained model forget specific knowledge, often for privacy or safety reasons (Wang et al., 2024a). Prior work has studied removing the influence of selected training examples (Chourasia and Shah, 2023; Jang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024b) or suppressing specific concepts from model representations (Belrose et al., 2023; Gandikota et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). However, most unlearning methods require access to model parameters or additional training, making them difficult to apply to black-box LLMs. Prompted Knowledge Cutoff (Gao et al., 2025) offers a lightweight alternative by using prompts to make an LLM behave as if it had forgotten in-formation after a specified cutoff year. This frame-work requires neither parameter updates nor model-internal access, making it practical for black-box LLMs. Gao et al. (2025) evaluate prompted cut-off behavior on three settings: Factual, which tests direct facts whose answers change over time; Se-mantic, which tests words whose meanings shift across years; and Counterfactual, which tests ques-tions where post-cutoff events causally affect the answer. Their results show that prompted cutoff is relatively effective when the target knowledge is di-rectly queried, but degrades substantially in Coun-terfactual settings where the post-cutoff knowledge is only causally related to the question. Our work builds on this finding by introducing recall-based prompting strategies and a multi-cutoff benchmark to better evaluate robustness across different cutoff year settings. 

B Benchmark Details 

This section provides additional details on the four benchmarks used in our experiments: the three datasets from Gao et al. (2025), which are dis-tributed under the Apache License 2.0, and our Multi-cutoff Historical Event Benchmark (MHEB). The Factual, Semantic, Counterfactual, and MHEB datasets contain 675, 303, 689, and 900 examples, respectively. 

B.1 Factual 

The model is asked a binary-choice question about direct factual changes in the state of the world, such as a person, institution, or event at a particular year. The evaluation checks whether the model’s final answer matches the correct answer under the specified cutoff year. One option corresponds to the correct answer without applying a knowledge cutoff (Answer before unlearn), while the other cor-responds to the correct answer after applying the cutoff (Answer after unlearn). The knowledge cut-off success rate is the proportion of cases in which the model selects the cutoff-consistent answer. A example from the Factual dataset is shown below:

B.2 Semantic 

The model is asked to define words whose mean-ings have changed over time, such as words with semantic shifts or neologisms. The evaluation mea-sures whether the model can produce the meaning corresponding to the cutoff year (Meaning after un-learn) under the knowledge cutoff setting. Unlike Factual, the model generates a free-form textual definition of the given word. B.3 Counterfactual. 

The model is asked to infer the outcome that would have occurred if it did not know unexpected post-cutoff events, such as postponements, cancellations, or external shocks. Each question is given in a four-choice format. The evaluation checks whether the model’s final answer matches the correct answer under the knowledge state at the cutoff year. The options include both the answer without applying a knowledge cutoff (Answer before unlearn) and the answer after applying the cutoff (Answer after unlearn). We report two success rates: Hard , which counts a case as successful only when the model selects the correct cutoff-consistent answer, and 

Soft , which counts a case as successful when the model does not select the no-cutoff answer. An example from the Counterfactual dataset is shown below. 

> Example of the Counterfactual dataset.
> “Question”: “Which country hosted the 2015 Africa Cup of Nations?”, “Options”: {“A”: “Equatorial Guinea”, “B”: “Mo-rocco”, “C”: “South Africa”, “D”: “Nigeria”}, “Answer before unlearn”: “A”, “Answer after unlearn”: “B”, “Cutoff year”: “2010”

For the Factual and Counterfactual datasets, we additionally verify whether each model can correctly answer every question without a cutoff prompt. If any model fails on a question under this no-cutoff setting, we exclude that question from evaluation. This ensures that cutoff failures reflect the model’s difficulty in suppressing post-cutoff knowledge rather than a lack of relevant factual knowledge. 

B.4 MHEB Details 

The existing prompted knowledge cutoff bench-mark (Gao et al., 2025) evaluates each question under a single cutoff year, making it difficult to study how performance changes across different cutoff settings. To address this limitation, we con-struct the Multi-cutoff Historical Event Benchmark (MHEB), which evaluates the same question under multiple cutoff years. We first collect historical events from Wikidata, focusing on event-related entities categorized as 

war (Q198), battle (Q178561), or occurrence 

(Q1190554). Each record consists of an event name and its occurrence year. To ensure that the target model already knows the event, we ask it to iden-tify the occurrence year of each candidate event without any cutoff instruction, using the prompt shown below. We retain only events for which the model correctly predicts the year. 

> Prompt for verifying the event year.
> You are a helpful assistant that answers questions about history. When asked about a year, you MUST answer only with the four-digit year, with no other text. In what year did {event} occur (or begin)? Please answer with the four-digit year only.

Using the retained events, we construct MHEB instances following the Factual benchmark format of Gao et al. (2025), as shown below. Each instance asks whether a historical event has ever occurred, with {event} and {event year} replaced by the cor-responding Wikidata event name and occurrence year. 

> MHEB instance format.
> “Question”: “Has a historical event called {event} ever occurred?” “Answer before unlearn”: “Yes” “Answer after unlearn”: “No” “Answer switch year”: {event year}

The final dataset contains 900 historical events, with 100 events sampled from each century starting from the 13th century (1201–1300). For each event, we evaluate cutoff years from the event year back to nine years before the event year. The correct answer is “Yes” when no cutoff is applied or when the cutoff year is later than the event year, and “No” when the cutoff year is the same as or earlier than the event year. 

C Implementation Details 

We evaluate three LLMs in our experiments: GPT-4o 2 (Hurst et al., 2024), gpt-oss-120b 3 (Agar-wal et al., 2025) with MXFP4 quantization, and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct 4 (Grattafiori et al., 2024) with BF16 weight precision. GPT-4o is accessed through the OpenAI API 5, gpt-oss-120b through the Together API 6, and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct is run locally on NVIDIA H100 96GB GPUs. For all models, we set the temperature to 0 and use a fixed random seed when applicable.

F Ethical Considerations 

This work studies prompt-based strategies for en-hancing knowledge cutoff behavior in LLMs. The datasets used in our experiments are either released by Gao et al. (2025) or constructed from publicly available Wikidata entries, which helps reduce po-tential ethical concerns to some extent. Our methods rely on the LLM’s own underlying knowledge and therefore may inherit its biases or hallucinations (Hida et al., 2024; Bang et al., 2025). In addition, the proposed recall-based prompting can reduce post-cutoff leakage but cannot guaran-tee true forgetting. We therefore encourage users to apply our method with caution, especially in settings where reliable information control is re-quired. P1
