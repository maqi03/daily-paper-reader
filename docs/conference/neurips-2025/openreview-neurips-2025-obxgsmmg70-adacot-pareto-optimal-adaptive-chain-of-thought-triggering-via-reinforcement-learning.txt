## Abstract 

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities but 1

often face challenges with tasks requiring sophisticated reasoning. While Chain-2

of-Thought (CoT) prompting significantly enhances reasoning, it indiscriminately 3

generates lengthy reasoning steps for all queries, leading to substantial compu-4

tational costs and inefficiency, especially for simpler inputs. To address this 5

critical issue, we introduce AdaCoT (Adaptive Chain-of-Thought), a novel frame-6

work enabling LLMs to adaptively decide when to invoke CoT. AdaCoT framed 7

adaptive reasoning as a Pareto optimization problem that seeks to balance model 8

performance with the costs associated with CoT invocation (both frequency and 9

computational overhead). We propose a reinforcement learning (RL) based method, 10 

specifically utilizing Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), to dynamically control 11 

the CoT triggering decision boundary by adjusting penalty coefficients, thereby 12 

allowing the model to determine CoT necessity based on implicit query complex-13 

ity. A key technical contribution is Selective Loss Masking (SLM), designed to 14 

counteract decision boundary collapse during multi-stage RL training, ensuring 15 

robust and stable adaptive triggering. Experimental results demonstrate that Ada-16 

CoT successfully navigates the Pareto frontier, achieving substantial reductions 17 

in CoT usage for queries not requiring elaborate reasoning. For instance, on our 18 

production traffic testset, AdaCoT reduced CoT triggering rates to as low as 3.18% 19 

and decreased average response tokens by 69.06%, while maintaining high perfor-20 

mance on complex tasks. This substantial token decrease directly translates to a 21 

significant reduction in inference computational load. AdaCoT pioneers adaptive 22 

CoT triggering, offering a practical and principled solution for developing more 23 

efficient, responsive, and cost-effective LLMs, particularly crucial for interactive 24 

and resource-sensitive applications. 25 

## 1 Introduction 26 

Large Language Models (LLMs) have garnered substantial attention due to their remarkable ability 27 

to encode extensive world knowledge from vast corpora [ 1], enabling impressive performance 28 

across diverse tasks such as question answering, creative writing, and summarization. Despite 29 

these successes, LLMs often demonstrate limitations in tasks requiring sophisticated reasoning, 30 

including solving complex mathematical problems and intricate coding puzzles. To mitigate this, 31 

recent methodologies have employed Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting [ 2 ], which encourages 32 

models to explicitly generate step-by-step reasoning prior to producing final answers. This approach 33 

significantly enhances the reasoning capability of models, even achieving human-expert levels in 34 

certain domains [3, 4, 5, 6]. 35 

Submitted to 39th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2025). Do not distribute. However, employing CoT prompting also poses critical challenges during inference [ 7]. Specifically, 36 

it substantially increases the number of tokens generated, even for simple queries that do not benefit 37 

from elaborate reasoning, such as straightforward arithmetic questions. This indiscriminate token 38 

expense consequently raises deployment costs and reduces inference efficiency. Ideally, a model 39 

should adaptively determine when detailed reasoning is necessary. For instance, simple queries like 40 

"What is 1+1?" should be answered immediately without additional reasoning steps, whereas more 41 

complex queries require deeper and step-by-step reasoning. An adaptive strategy would thus optimize 42 

token usage, balancing cost-efficiency with response quality. 43 

Recently, a few efforts has made attempts towards this direction. These approaches can be broadly 44 

categorized into three main directions. (1) Incorporate length penalties or brevity rewards during 45 

the reinforcement learning (RL) stage to encourage shorter, more concise reasoning paths [ 8, 9 , 10 ,46 

11 , 12 , 13 ]. (2) Restructure CoT outputs through post-processing such as iterative summarization 47 

or pruning [ 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 ]. (3) Employ explicit user instructions or hand-crafted 48 

selection mechanisms to control the use of CoT [ 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 ]. Despite their contributions, 49 

they mainly focus on monotonic reasoning reduction, failing to account for the nuanced variability 50 

in query complexity, i.e., treating simple and difficult prompts adaptively. Moreover, they lack a 51 

principled optimization framework to guide balancing response quality against deployment cost. 52 

To address these limitations, we introduce AdaCoT (Adaptive Chain-of-Thought), a novel approach 53 

grounded in formal mathematical analysis. Our key insight is framing adaptive reasoning as a 54 

multi-objective optimization problem with two competing goals: maximizing response accuracy and 55 

minimizing deployment costs. Specifically, we formalize this balance through Pareto optimization, 56 

seeking optimal trade-offs between reasoning complexity and inference efficiency. Such a mathemati-57 

cal framework provides clear theoretical grounding for dynamically adapting CoT triggering based 58 

on query complexity. 59 

Leveraging this formalization, we propose an RL-based strategy explicitly designed around the Pareto 60 

optimization framework, enabling effective control of the model’s decision boundary for initiating 61 

CoT prompting. During training, the RL agent dynamically assesses the complexity of incoming 62 

user queries to determine the necessity and extent of reasoning steps. By carefully designing the 63 

reward function to incorporate penalty coefficients, we encourage the RL agent to seek solutions 64 

along the Pareto frontier, explicitly optimizing trade-offs between response accuracy and token 65 

expenditure. This structured exploration enables the model to effectively discern when detailed 66 

reasoning is beneficial, thereby systematically enhancing inference efficiency and significantly 67 

reducing deployment costs. 68 

The proposed AdaCoT framework delivers substantial benefits in LLM operational efficiency. By 69 

empowering models to selectively engage CoT, AdaCoT can reduce triggering rates to as low as 70 

3.18% and cut average response tokens by 69.1% in production settings. This significant reduction 71 

in computational load is achieved while maintaining strong performance on 15 widely-adopted 72 

benchmarks. For example, AdaCoT can achieve a 62.8% average score using only a 53.3% CoT rate, 73 

closely rivaling the 65.0% score of a model that always employs CoT. These improvements directly 74 

translate to more cost-effective and responsive LLM systems. 75 

## 2 The AdaCoT Framework 76 

Enabling a large language model (LLM) to dynamically decide whether to invoke Chain-of-Thought 77 

(CoT) reasoning based on the complexity of user queries is a critical task, which allows LLMs to 78 

allocate computational resources more rationally, i.e., spending tokens on complex reasoning tasks 79 

while avoiding unnecessary overhead for simple queries. 80 

To achieve this, we introduce AdaCoT, a unified framework for adaptive reasoning. The central 81 

insight is that the decision to employ CoT prompting can be naturally cast as an optimization problem 82 

involving two competing goals: (1) maximizing response accuracy and (2) minimizing deployment 83 

costs. To capture this trade-off, we frame the task as a Pareto optimization problem, providing a 84 

principled foundation for balancing reasoning quality against computational efficiency. Based on this 85 

formulation, we propose an RL-based control strategy that governs the invocation of CoT reasoning. 86 

Specifically, we train a policy model learning to assess the complexity of each query and decide 87 

whether CoT reasoning should be used. During RL training, the policy model is optimized using a 88 

reward signal carefully designed to reflect the Pareto trade-off, incorporating both task performance 89 

2and token efficiency. Through this RL-based mechanism, AdaCoT learns to allocate reasoning effort 90 

adaptively, yielding high-quality responses with minimal computational cost. 91

2.2 Training Pipeline for AdaCoT 110 

The AdaCoT training pipeline integrates supervised fine-tuning (SFT) as an initialization phase, 111 

followed by multi-stage reinforcement learning (RL) to refine the adaptive CoT triggering behavior. 112 

2.2.1 Data Preparation and Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) as Warm-up 113 

To provide the model with an initial understanding of when CoT might be beneficial, we perform 114 

a data preparation stage. This is achieved by leveraging an auxiliary model, guided by a set of 115 

predefined principles (e.g., query complexity, expected reasoning depth, domain; see Appendix B). In 116 

our implementation, we use an internal 15B-parameter model to generate these annotations; however, 117 

the framework is model-agnostic and can be instantiated using any sufficiently capable LLM with 118 

basic instruction-following abilities. Queries are labeled as either likely benefiting from CoT or 119 

likely suitable for a direct answer. This principled, automated labeling process is more consistent and 120 

scalable than manual annotation. 121 

The SFT training data is then structured as follows: For queries labeled as benefiting from CoT, 122 

responses retain the full reasoning process: <think>reasoning_steps</think>answer . For 123 

queries labeled as not requiring CoT, responses omit explicit reasoning but maintain structural 124 

consistency: <think></think>answer . This SFT stage serves as a "warm-up", equipping the 125 

model with a foundational capability to distinguish between these two response styles. The consistent 126 

use of <think></think> tags is crucial for maintaining response format integrity. 127 

2.2.2 Reinforcement Learning for Adaptive CoT Control 128 

The RL stage is pivotal for fine-tuning AdaCoT’s adaptive reasoning capabilities. We design a reward 129 

function R(x, r ) for an input query x and generated response r:130 

R(x, r ) = Rbase (x, r ) − α1 · Pmiss (x, r ) − α2 · Pover (x, r ) − γ · Pfmt (r) (4) 3where Rbase (x, r ) is the base reward reflecting response quality, Pmiss (x, r ) is a binary penalty for 131 

reasoning omission, Pover (x, r ) is a binary penalty for reasoning overuse, Pfmt (r) is a binary penalty 132 

for format errors, and α1, α 2, γ are non-negative penalty coefficients. By adjusting α1 and α2, we 133 

steer AdaCoT towards different CoT triggering decision boundaries, allowing exploration of the 134 

Pareto frontier. 135 

2.2.3 Addressing Decision Boundary Collapse with Selective Loss Masking 136 

A significant challenge in multi-stage RL, particularly when fine-tuning on specialized datasets with 137 

skewed CoT distributions (e.g., mathematical datasets where CoT is almost always beneficial), is the 138 

risk of the adaptive CoT triggering capability becoming unstable or collapsing. The model might 139 

revert to a homogeneous behavior, either always or never triggering CoT, thereby losing the nuanced 140 

decision-making learned in earlier, more balanced training stages. We term this phenomenon decision 141 

boundary collapse. This is particularly problematic if the final RL stage has significant bias, as it can 142 

lead to the model almost completely losing its adaptive triggering capability. 143 

To address decision boundary collapse, AdaCoT incorporates Selective Loss Masking ( SLM ). SLM 144 

aims to preserve the CoT triggering ratio and distribution established during SFT or prior RL stages. It 145 

achieves this by selectively masking the loss contribution from the pivotal "decision token" during RL 146 

phases prone to distribution bias. This decision token is defined as the token immediately succeeding 147 

the <think> tag. 148

## 3 Experiments 153 

We conducted extensive experiments to evaluate the AdaCoT framework, focusing on its ability to 154 

navigate the performance-cost trade-off, the effectiveness of its adaptive triggering mechanism, and 155 

its impact on inference efficiency. This section details our experimental setup, presents the main 156 

results, and analyzes the findings. 157 

3.1 Experimental Setup 158 

For our base model, we utilized our internal 15B/150B parameter Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) [ 28 , 29 ]159 

model. The AdaCoT post-training process comprised an initial SFT stage, followed by a two-stage 160 

RL procedure: first, a Mathematics-Focused RL stage (RL-Math) concentrated on complex, rule-161 

verifiable problems, and second, a General Domain RL stage (RL-General) which incorporated 162 

broader data and a trained reward model. We compared our AdaCoT RL Models (Exp1-Exp4) 163 

against several baselines: a Full CoT SFT Baseline (SFT model always generating CoT), a Full 164 

CoT RL Baseline (RL model derived from the Full CoT SFT, always generating CoT), a No CoT 165 

SFT Baseline (SFT model never generating CoT), a No CoT RL Baseline (RL model derived from 166 

the No CoT SFT, never generating CoT), and our AdaCoT SFT Model (our model after only the 167 

SFT stage, also referred to as Adaptive SFT Model). 168 

The SFT and RL training datasets were constructed to cover a diverse range of domains, including 169 

mathematics, reasoning, professional disciplines (e.g., law, medicine), dialogue, creative writing, and 170 

general knowledge question answering. Both SFT and RL data were labeled for CoT necessity using 171 

the principle-guided assessment detailed in Appendix B. In the SFT dataset, approximately 67% of 172 

the samples were labeled as requiring CoT, while in the RL dataset, this proportion was around 40%. 173 

During SFT, queries identified as not requiring CoT were formatted with empty <think></think> 174 

tags. In the RL-Math stage, which is particularly prone to decision boundary collapse, we employed 175 

Selective Loss Masking (SLM), as described in Section 2.2.3. For the RL-General stage, we applied 176 

penalties according to Equation 4, systematically varying the α1 and α2 coefficients to explore 177 

different points on the Pareto frontier. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [ 30 ] was used for all RL 178 

policy updates. 179

3.2 Results and Analysis 195 

Our results demonstrate AdaCoT’s ability to effectively control CoT invocation, leading to improved 196 

efficiency while maintaining strong performance. 197 

3.2.1 Pareto Frontier Analysis 198 

We trained four variants of our AdaCoT RL model (Exp1-Exp4) by varying the penalty coefficients α1199 

(for missing CoT) and α2 (for overusing CoT). The specific coefficients were: Exp1 ( α1 = 0 .1, α 2 =200 

0.3), Exp2 ( α1 = 0 .2, α 2 = 0 .3), Exp3 ( α1 = 0 .3, α 2 = 0 .3), and Exp4 ( α1 = 0 .3, α 2 = 0 .1). 201 

The format error penalty γ was consistently set to 1.0. Figure 1 illustrates the average score plotted 202 

against the CoT triggering rate for these models and the baselines, based on the average performance 203 

across our 15 benchmark datasets (detailed in Appendix Table 4). The No CoT SFT baseline achieved 204 

an average score of 43.6% with 0% CoT usage, while the No CoT RL baseline improved this to 205 

47.7% at 0% CoT. The AdaCoT SFT Model (Adaptive SFT) registered a 57.1% average score at a 206 

61.3% CoT rate. 207 

The AdaCoT RL models trace a compelling Pareto frontier. AdaCoT RL Exp1 (43.1% CoT, 59.7% 208 

score) and AdaCoT RL Exp2 (53.3% CoT, 62.8% score) demonstrate significant performance gains 209 

over the AdaCoT SFT model while operating at lower or comparable CoT rates. Notably, AdaCoT 210 

RL Exp2 achieves a 62.8% average score, approaching the Full CoT RL baseline (65.0% score, 211 

100% CoT) with nearly half the CoT usage. As we increase the CoT triggering rate, AdaCoT RL 212 

Exp3 (65.4% CoT, 64.3% score) and AdaCoT RL Exp4 (67.7% CoT, 64.4% score) further push 213 

performance, closely rivaling the Full CoT RL baseline’s score but with approximately 32-35% less 214 

CoT invocation. Moreover, these results indicate that despite the fixed CoT triggering labels within 215 

the SFT/RL data, adjusting the combination of penalty coefficients during the RL phase enables the 216 

final RL model to learn triggering strategies that transcend these initial labeling constraints. 217 

This highlights AdaCoT’s effectiveness in navigating the trade-off between performance and CoT cost. 218 

However, it is also observable that while the AdaCoT RL models achieve substantial efficiency gains 219 

and define a superior Pareto curve compared to simpler baselines, they encounter a slight performance 220 

bottleneck when their triggering rates are pushed higher. Specifically, even the highest-performing 221 

adaptive models (Exp3 and Exp4, with scores of 64.3% and 64.4% respectively) do not surpass the 222 

average score of the Full CoT RL baseline (65.0%). This suggests that while AdaCoT excels at 223 

reducing CoT for a vast majority of queries without compromising much on average performance, 224 

and indeed offers a better score-to-cost ratio, the absolute peak average performance achieved by 225 

a model specialized to always use CoT (Full CoT RL) remains marginally higher. This indicates 226 

that the adaptive mechanism, while highly effective, might not perfectly replicate or exceed the 227 

performance of an always-on CoT strategy in every single scenario contributing to the average, thus 228 

not fully crossing this specific optimal boundary for maximum average score. This could be due to 229 

the inherent complexities of learning a universally optimal triggering heuristic or the RL optimization 230 

finding a balance that prioritizes the significant cost savings available across the query distribution. 231

## 4 Discussion and Future Work 256 

4.1 Design Considerations and Limitations 257 

AdaCoT offers a pragmatic approach to adaptive reasoning by combining principle-guided initial data 258 

labeling with RL-based optimization of the CoT decision boundary. This methodology was chosen 259 

to circumvent inherent challenges in purely autonomous CoT trigger learning, such as information 260 

asymmetry in assessing counterfactual benefits and the difficulty of quantifying quality degradation 261 

from CoT omission, particularly for subjective tasks. 262 

While AdaCoT is a promising initial step, several limitations exist. The optimal CoT triggering 263 

strategy is relative to the base model’s capabilities, necessitating recalibration for different LLMs. 264 

Our current binary CoT invocation (on/off) simplifies a continuous spectrum of reasoning depths and 265 

styles, potentially limiting nuance. Domain generalization remains a challenge, as CoT necessity can 266 

vary significantly across knowledge areas, and the framework currently lacks personalization for user 267 

verbosity preferences. Moreover, the initial principle-guided labeling requires continuous refinement. 268 

Our Pareto analysis (Section 3.2.1) also indicates that while AdaCoT significantly improves efficiency 269 

and nears the peak average performance of specialized always-on CoT models, a small performance 270 

gap persists, highlighting the difficulty for adaptive mechanisms to achieve absolute maximum 271 

performance across all query types. 272 

Acknowledging the limitations of the current framework, we anticipate that future research will offer 273 

valuable critiques and further refine these initial explorations. Areas warranting deeper investigation 274 

include more granular control over reasoning, such as adaptive reasoning length where models 275 

dynamically adjust verbosity, or more nuanced triggering mechanisms beyond a simple binary 276 

decision. We believe such continued efforts by the community are crucial for developing more 277 

sophisticated and efficient reasoning strategies, potentially addressing the observed performance gap 278 

while maximizing efficiency and enhancing nuanced control over LLM reasoning. 279

4.3 Preserving Peak Performance with AdaCoT 306 

A critical question is whether adaptive reasoning limits a model’s maximum performance. We 307 

investigated this using System Prompts (SPs), integrated into AdaCoT’s SFT and RL training to 308 

control reasoning behavior (e.g., "Always Reason SP," "Never Reason SP"). During SFT, a small 309 

portion of data was augmented with SPs, and target responses were modified for compliance. In 310 

RL, a fraction of training prompts included SPs, with rewards penalizing deviations from explicit SP 311 

instructions, ensuring robust adherence (details in Appendix C). Our focus here is using the "Always 312 

Reason SP" to assess AdaCoT’s performance ceiling. 313 

Instructing AdaCoT RL models to always generate CoT via this SP allowed direct comparison against 314 

the Full CoT RL Baseline on our 15 benchmark datasets. As shown in Table 3, AdaCoT RL models 315 

in this forced "Always Reason" mode achieved average scores that were highly competitive with, 316 

and in instances like AdaCoT RL Model Exp4 (65.7) and Exp2 (65.3), slightly surpassed the Full 317 

CoT RL Baseline (65.0). This demonstrates a key strength: AdaCoT’s adaptive training, aimed 318 

at optimizing the performance-cost Pareto frontier, does not inherently restrict the model’s peak 319 

reasoning capabilities when comprehensive reasoning is explicitly demanded. While our Pareto 320 

analysis (Section 3.2.1) noted a slight gap in average scores when models operate adaptively, these 321 

SP-controlled results affirm that AdaCoT offers efficiency without sacrificing potential high-end 322 

performance. 323

## 5 Related Work 333 

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning [ 2] significantly advanced LLM capabilities by prompting step-334 

by-step thought processes, inspiring sophisticated strategies like diverse path sampling [ 44 ] or 335 

structured thoughts (trees [ 45 ], graphs [ 46 ]). However, CoT’s verbosity and cost [ 47 , 7] are major 336 

drawbacks. The varying utility of CoT—direct answers being better for some queries [ 48 ] while 337 

complex tasks need longer reasoning [ 49 , 50 ]—underscores a critical cost-effectiveness trade-off, 338 

motivating research into efficient reasoning. 339 

Most existing work on CoT efficiency has focused on reducing reasoning length , rather than adap-340 

tively deciding whether to invoke CoT. These length reduction strategies include: (1) RL with length 341 

penalties or rewards for brevity [ 8, 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 ]; (2) Restructuring or compressing CoT content 342 

via learned compact representations, iterative summarization, cognitive paradigms, or dynamic termi-343 

nation [ 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 ]; (3) Employing explicit instructions or selection mechanisms 344 

for conciseness, such as dynamic token allocation, prompts for shorter responses, distillation, or 345 

selecting the shortest valid reasoning [22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. 346 

While effective for length, these methods generally do not equip a single model to dynamically 347 

decide CoT invocation based on query nature. Alternatives involve model merging [ 51 , 52 , 53 ] or 348 

manual CoT toggling. AdaCoT distinctively addresses adaptive triggering. By framing it as a Pareto 349 

optimization problem and using RL to control the CoT decision boundary, AdaCoT enables nuanced, 350 

context-dependent CoT invocation by a single model, filling a crucial gap towards truly efficient and 351 

versatile LLMs. 352 

## 6 Conclusion 353 

In this paper, we introduced AdaCoT, a novel framework for adaptive Chain-of-Thought reasoning 354 

in LLMs. By formulating adaptive reasoning as a Pareto optimization problem and employing an 355 

RL-based method with adjustable penalty coefficients, AdaCoT dynamically controls CoT triggering 356 

based on implicit query complexity. Our experiments demonstrate AdaCoT’s success in navigating 357 

the Pareto frontier, achieving substantial reductions in CoT usage—and thereby computational costs 358 

and latency—for simpler queries, while preserving high performance on complex reasoning tasks. 359 

The introduction of Selective Loss Masking effectively ensures robust adaptive triggering throughout 360 

multi-stage RL training. Distinguishing itself from prior work predominantly focused on CoT length 361 

compression, AdaCoT pioneers adaptive CoT triggering, offering a practical and principled solution 362 

for developing more efficient, responsive, and cost-effective LLMs, particularly crucial for interactive 363 

applications. 364

## E Training and Testing Details 1222 

This appendix provides further details on the experimental and testing configurations used in our 1223 

study, complementing the setup described in Section 3.1 of the main paper. We outline the specifics 1224 

of our Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Reward Model (RM) training, Reinforcement Learning (RL) 1225 

training, and evaluation settings. 1226 

SFT Training 1227 

We utilized a pre-trained model with a 15B/150B Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture as our base 1228 

model. All training cases were truncated to a maximum of 32,000 tokens. We employed a cosine 1229 

26 decay learning rate schedule, with the peak learning rate set to 2 × 10 −5, gradually decaying to 1230 

2 × 10 −6.1231 

RM Training 1232 

The Reward Model (RM) was initialized using the SFT model and subsequently trained on a diverse 1233 

set of internally, human-annotated data. 1234 

RL Training 1235 

The dataset for Reinforcement Learning (RL) training was composed of two main types: 1236 

• Verifiable data , which receives feedback from a verifier. This type of data allows for direct 1237 

validation of the model’s outputs against known criteria. 1238 

• General data , scored by our reward model. The reward model assigns scores based on how 1239 

well the model’s responses align with human preferences. 1240 

Testing 1241 

For all evaluations, the inference temperature was set to 1.0 and top-p sampling was set to 0.7. Each 1242 

test case was inferred at least 5 times, and the average score across these inferences was reported as 1243 

the result for that case. 1244 

Note on Data and Setup Disclosure 1245 

We strive to be as transparent as possible regarding our methodology. However, due to proprietary 1246 

considerations and company confidentiality policies, we are unable to disclose further specifics 1247 

about the training dataset composition or more granular details of the training setup at this time. We 1248 

appreciate the understanding of the research community and hope that the provided information is 1249 

sufficient to contextualize our findings and facilitate the reproducibility of our core concepts. We are 1250 

committed to contributing to the open exchange of scientific knowledge within the bounds of these 1251
